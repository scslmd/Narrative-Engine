## The Writing Process: 6 Stages

Research across all sources converges on a consistent 6-stage process. Authors do not follow a strictly linear path — they loop, backtrack, and iterate. The application must support non-linear navigation between stages.

### Stage 1: Ideation & Concept Development

**Activities:**
- Brainstorming the "BIG idea" — the core concept that drives the narrative
- Defining genre, tone, and target audience
- Exploring premise variations ("What if...?" scenarios)
- Assessing concept viability: Does it have legs? Will it sustain a full manuscript?
- Initial character sketches and world concepts

**Key Insight (Jenkins):** The idea must capture the writer so completely that they're *compelled* to write it. Without genuine passion for the concept, the writer will lose interest during the Marathon of the Middle.

**Tools Used:**
- Brainstorm notebooks / digital notes
- Idea journals for capturing inspiration
- Prompt generators for writer's block
- Mind mapping tools for concept exploration

**Application Implications:**
- Brainstorm panel for idea capture and exploration
- Premise builder to distill concepts to single-sentence hooks
- Genre/tone selector to frame the writing context
- Idea viability assessment prompts

### Stage 2: Planning & Outlining

**Activities:**
- Constructing the outline — the directional document that provides structure
- Mapping plot points against classic story structures (Three-Act, Save the Cat, Hero's Journey, etc.)
- Developing character profiles (goals, arcs, motivations, contradictions)
- Building the world bible (rules, geography, history, magic systems)
- Establishing chapter-by-chapter beat sheets
- Research planning — identifying what needs to be researched before/during drafting

**Key Insight (Jenkins):** Even "Pantsers" (discovery writers) need at least a basic structure. The outline serves as a safety net when the writing gets difficult. For nonfiction, the outline is non-negotiable.

**Key Insight (Reedsy):** Outlines are fluid documents — they evolve during the writing process. The application should support outline modification without breaking existing work.

**Tools Used:**
- Scrivener (most popular professional tool)
- Index cards / cork boards for scene organization
- Spreadsheet trackers for timeline consistency
- Worldbuilding templates (130-question frameworks)
- Character profile questionnaires

**Application Implications:**
- Outline editor with drag-and-drop scene reordering
- Character profile system with arc tracking
- World bible with cross-referencing capabilities
- Timeline visualization tool
- Research task list integrated with scenes/chapters

### Stage 3: Research

**Activities vary significantly by genre:**

| Genre | Research Needs | Tools & Sources |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Historical Fiction** | Period-accurate details, clothing, speech patterns, technology, social customs | Archives, historical texts, museum resources, academic papers |
| **Science Fiction** | Scientific principles, technology speculation, world-building consistency | Scientific journals, expert consultations, physics/math references |
| **Fantasy** | Mythology, cultural systems, magic system rules, geography | Folklore collections, comparative religion, cartography tools |
| **Mystery/Thriller** | Forensic procedures, criminal psychology, investigation methods | Police manuals, crime databases, expert interviews |
| **Romance** | Relationship dynamics, cultural contexts, emotional authenticity | Psychology research, cultural studies, emotional intelligence frameworks |
| **Literary Fiction** | Thematic depth, character psychology, stylistic precision | Literary criticism, philosophy, human behavior studies |
| **Nonfiction** | Subject matter expertise, fact verification, source credibility | Academic databases, primary sources, expert interviews |

**Key Insight (Jenkins):** Research is vital for both fiction and nonfiction. Fiction requires accurate details for believability. Nonfiction requires factual accuracy for credibility. "The last thing you want is even a small mistake due to your lack of proper research."

**Research Process:**
1. **Pre-writing research** — background reading to understand the subject/world
2. **Just-in-time research** — looking up specific details while drafting scenes
3. **Revision research** — filling gaps discovered during editing
4. **Fact-checking** — verifying details before submission

**Tools Used:**
- World Almanacs for factual data
- Thesaurus (Merriam-Webster) for word choice
- Online encyclopedias (Wikipedia, WorldAtlas)
- Academic databases (JSTOR, Google Scholar)
- Expert consultations and interviews
- Reference books and primary sources

**Application Implications:**
- Research repository linked to scenes/chapters
- Genre-specific research templates
- Citation tracking for nonfiction
- Research notes integrated into world bible
- Fact-checking flags for revision stage

### Stage 4: Drafting

**Activities:**
- Writing the first draft — focusing on getting the story down, not perfection
- Maintaining daily/weekly writing goals (typically 300-1000 words/day)
- Following the outline while allowing for organic discovery
- Managing the "Marathon of the Middle" — sustaining momentum through the longest stretch
- Writing without internal editing (separating creation from revision)

**Key Insight (Vanderkam/The Atlantic):** "Write fast, edit slow." The first draft is a slab of meat that will be carved later. Writing 4,000 words of rough draft is easier than creating perfect prose from nothing.

**Key Insight (Jenkins):** Turn off the internal editor during first draft. "I cannot be both creator and editor at the same time. That slows me to a crawl." Many writers alternate: heavy edit of previous day's work each morning, then new drafting.

**Drafting Strategies:**
- **Scheduled writing** — 6+ hours per week, consistent times/days
- **Word/page goals** — daily targets to maintain progress
- **Deadline pressure** — sacred deadlines create accountability
- **Distraction elimination** — blocking apps, focused environments
- **Embracing procrastination** — subconscious processing is part of the creative process

**Tools Used:**
- Word processors (Microsoft Word, Google Docs)
- Distraction-free writing apps (FocusWriter, WriteRoom)
- Writing trackers (word count, streak tracking)
- Timer apps for focused writing sessions
- Scrivener for long-form organization

**Application Implications:**
- Distraction-free writing interface
- Word count goals and progress tracking
- Writing session timer with stats
- Scene-by-scene drafting view
- Outline integration with draft text
- Auto-save and version history

### Stage 5: Revision & Refinement

**The revision process is iterative and multi-layered:**

**Layer 1: Structural Revision (Big Picture)**
- Read through entire manuscript with fresh eyes
- Map timeline and check for contradictions
- Assess overall pacing — does the story flow?
- Identify plot holes and logical gaps
- Evaluate character arcs — do characters change meaningfully?
- Check worldbuilding consistency

**Layer 2: Character Development**
- Round out flat characters with unique speech patterns, habits, quirks
- Ensure character motivations are clear and consistent
- Verify character voices are distinct
- Add depth through backstory and internal conflict
- Fill out character profiles to discover new dimensions

**Layer 3: Scene-Level Revision**
- Hone scene-by-scene pacing
- Improve dialogue — does it advance plot or deepen character?
- Strengthen transitions between scenes
- Add sensory details and vivid description
- Cut unnecessary scenes or combine overlapping ones

**Layer 4: Line Editing**
- Eliminate unnecessary words
- Fix repetitive phrases and bad habits
- Improve sentence variety and rhythm
- Check for consistency (eye color, timeline details, etc.)
- Remove clichés and purple prose

**Key Insight (Reedsy):** "Getting space from your manuscript is key to putting yourself in a 'reader' mindset." Wait at least a few weeks between finishing the draft and beginning revision.

**Key Insight (Jenkins):** You know you're finished revising when you've gone from making your writing *better* to merely making it *different*.

**Tools Used:**
- Highlighters for marking issue types by color
- Printouts for manual annotation
- Spreadsheet trackers for character/plot continuity
- Grammar/style checkers (ProWritingAid, Grammarly)
- Beta reader feedback forms

**Application Implications:**
- Multiple revision passes with different focus areas
- Timeline visualization for consistency checking
- Character arc tracker across scenes
- Scene pacing analyzer
- Continuity checker (flags contradictions)
- Revision notes attached to specific passages
- Beta reader feedback integration

### Stage 6: Final Polish & Preparation

**Activities:**
- Copy editing — final grammar, spelling, punctuation pass
- Manuscript formatting (12pt serif font, double-spaced, proper margins)
- Creating query letters or book proposals (for traditional publishing)
- Preparing manuscript for submission or self-publishing
- Final proofread — reading aloud to catch awkward phrasing

**Key Insight (wikiHow):** "Keep revising your book, but don't make excuses for not submitting it." At some point, the manuscript must be released to the process.

**Tools Used:**
- Style guides (Chicago Manual of Style, AP Style)
- Manuscript formatting templates
- Query letter generators
- Beta reader networks
- Professional editing services

**Application Implications:**
- Manuscript formatting export options
- Query letter builder
- Submission tracker for publishers/agents
- Final checklist before submission

## Author Workflows: 4 Entry Points

Research shows authors don't follow one linear process. They enter the writing process at different points based on how they think:

| Entry Point | Description | Common Genres | Primary Tools Needed |
|-------------|-------------|---------------|----------------------|
| **Idea-First (Pantser)** | Starts with a concept or "what if?" — writes organically, discovers story through writing | Romance, Literary Fiction | Ideas, Manuscripts, Characters |
| **Character-First** | Builds characters deeply first — their voices, motivations, relationships. Characters drive the plot. | Literary Fiction, Character-Driven Drama | Characters, Relationships, Arcs, Ideas |
| **Outline-First (Plotter)** | Plans everything — structure, beats, chapter outlines — before writing a word | Thriller, Mystery, Series Fiction | Structure, Chapters, Generation |
| **World-First** | Builds the world bible first — rules, geography, history, magic systems. Story emerges from world. | Fantasy, Science Fiction | World Bible, Characters, Arcs, Structure |

**Key Insight:** Authors naturally move between activities during a writing session. The workspace must support fluid transitions without losing context.

## Genre-Specific Research Patterns

Different genres require different research approaches and tools:

### Historical Fiction
- **Primary research:** Archives, period documents, museum collections
- **Secondary research:** Academic histories, biographies, scholarly articles
- **Verification:** Cross-referencing dates, events, cultural details
- **Tools:** Timeline builders, period reference libraries, map overlays

### Science Fiction
- **Primary research:** Scientific papers, technical manuals, expert consultations
- **Secondary research:** Science journalism, popular science books
- **Worldbuilding:** Physics rules, technology trees, alien biology
- **Tools:** Scientific calculators, reference databases, consistency checkers

### Fantasy
- **Primary research:** Mythology, folklore, comparative religion, linguistics
- **Secondary research:** Fantasy genre conventions, worldbuilding guides
- **Worldbuilding:** Magic systems, geography, cultural development
- **Tools:** Map makers, naming generators, magic system frameworks

### Mystery/Thriller
- **Primary research:** Crime scene procedures, forensic science, investigation methods
- **Secondary research:** True crime accounts, police manuals, psychological profiles
- **Plot construction:** Clue placement, red herring design, timeline mapping
- **Tools:** Clue trackers, timeline builders, suspect matrices

### Romance
- **Primary research:** Relationship psychology, cultural contexts, emotional dynamics
- **Secondary research:** Genre conventions, trope analysis, reader expectations
- **Character development:** Emotional arcs, chemistry building, conflict design
- **Tools:** Relationship trackers, emotional beat sheets, trope databases

### Literary Fiction
- **Primary research:** Thematic exploration, philosophical frameworks, human behavior
- **Secondary research:** Literary criticism, stylistic analysis, narrative techniques
- **Craft focus:** Voice development, prose style, structural experimentation
- **Tools:** Style analyzers, thematic mapping, narrative structure templates

### Nonfiction
- **Primary research:** Subject matter expertise, primary sources, interviews
- **Secondary research:** Academic literature, reference works, data sources
- **Organization:** Argument structure, evidence hierarchy, citation management
- **Tools:** Citation managers, fact-checking databases, outline builders

## Key Findings for Application Design

### 1. Non-Linear Workflow is Essential
Authors don't follow a strict linear process. They loop between stages, backtrack, and iterate. The application must support:
- Jumping between any stage without losing progress
- Simultaneous access to multiple tools (characters while drafting, research while outlining)
- Context preservation when switching activities

### 2. Research is Integrated, Not Separate
Research isn't a one-time activity — it's woven throughout the process:
- Pre-writing research informs concept development
- Just-in-time research supports drafting
- Revision research fills discovered gaps
- Fact-checking ensures accuracy before submission

### 3. Genre Dictates Process
Different genres require different tools, research approaches, and organizational structures:
- Historical fiction needs timeline builders and period references
- Fantasy needs worldbuilding templates and magic system frameworks
- Mystery needs clue trackers and plot construction tools
- Nonfiction needs citation management and argument structuring

### 4. Revision is Multi-Layered
Authors don't revise once — they revise in passes, each with different focus:
- Structural pass (big picture)
- Character pass (development, arcs)
- Scene pass (pacing, dialogue)
- Line edit pass (prose, style)
- Copy edit pass (grammar, mechanics)

### 5. Progress Tracking Matters
Authors need visibility into their progress:
- Word count goals and actuals
- Writing streaks and consistency
- Stage completion status
- Time invested per stage

### 6. Feedback Integration is Critical
The process includes multiple feedback loops:
- Self-assessment during drafting
- Beta reader feedback during revision
- Professional editing before submission
- Market feedback after publication

Based on research findings, the Narrative Engine should support:

1. **Brainstorm** — Idea capture, premise development, concept exploration

4. **Draft** — Distraction-free writing, goal tracking, scene management

- Historical Fiction: Timeline builder, period reference library
- Science Fiction: Science checker, technology tree builder
- Fantasy: Magic system framework, map maker, naming generator
- Mystery: Clue tracker, plot construction wizard, suspect matrix
